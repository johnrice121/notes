# The Degradation of the Digital Commons

[← Back to Index](./index.md)

Last updated: 2026-03-02

## Context

A growing public conversation about AI's effects on the Internet prompted me to think carefully about what is
actually at risk — and why. The fear that AI will inevitably collapse the Internet into useless noise is widespread
but imprecise. Pulling apart the mechanisms reveals a more nuanced and, in some ways, more troubling picture than
the common narrative suggests.

## Core Idea

AI is not the root cause of internet degradation. It is a force multiplier on economic and social incentive
structures that were already misaligned. The trajectory toward distrust, fragmentation, and epistemic inequality
is not technically inevitable. It is morally and institutionally contingent.

## Notes / Discussion

### Three Converging Vectors

What people loosely call "AI ruining the Internet" actually comprises three distinct failure modes operating on
the same substrate — the digital commons of shared reality and collective trust.

#### 1. AI Content Pollution ("Slop Collapse")

The fear: AI floods the internet with low-quality content, future models train on that degraded corpus, and
recursive quality decay spirals into collapse.

This is technically possible in poorly governed systems. It is not structurally inevitable. Frontier model training
uses curated corpora, deduplication, quality scoring, and mixed data sources. The concept of *model collapse* is
real and actively researched. The more realistic outcome is *signal stratification* — a widening gap between
high-signal curated ecosystems and open noise layers — rather than total entropy.

The concern has some validity. The collapse mechanism is usually overstated.

#### 2. Incentive-Driven Platform Decay

Platforms historically degrade not from AI but from economic logic: attract users, monetize users, extract rents,
degrade over time. This pattern is well-documented and predates AI entirely. AI accelerates it by lowering the
cost of engagement bait, filler content, and manipulation at scale.

The internet was already predominantly low-quality content before large language models existed. AI increases
velocity, not origin. The root variable is economic incentives, not model capability.

#### 3. Deepfakes and Epistemic Destabilization

This is the qualitatively different risk — and the more serious one.

AI-generated video and audio do not merely produce noise. They undermine the epistemological ground beneath shared
reality. Two destabilizing effects arise:

- *False positive*: fabricated content is believed to be real.
- *False negative*: real content is dismissed as fabricated.

The second is often more dangerous. If every damaging video can be credibly called AI-generated, accountability
weakens. This is not about quality decay. It is about the cost of forgery approaching zero while human perceptual
vulnerabilities remain constant. The democratization of forgery is the critical shift — not the existence of
forgery itself.

### Tragedy of the Commons

The Internet's shared resource is not bandwidth. It is *shared reality* and *collective trust*.

Individual actors — platforms, advertisers, content farms, political groups, even ordinary users chasing
engagement — benefit from extracting value from that commons. The costs are diffuse and externalized: cognitive
overload, polarization, distrust, epistemic uncertainty. No coordinated governance prevents exploitation at scale.

AI increases the efficiency of extraction. It does not change the underlying structure. The tragedy dynamic was
already present. AI accelerates the timeline.

### Tower of Babel

The Babel narrative is useful here. Fragmentation of shared language leads to breakdown of mutual intelligibility
and loss of coordinated collective action.

Applied to the Internet: when people can no longer agree on *whether events occurred* — not just how to interpret
them — institutional legitimacy weakens and cooperation decays. Deepfakes accelerate this dynamic. Algorithmic
feeds reinforce it by siloing people into epistemic tribes with incompatible factual frames. The result is not
merely disagreement. It is *ontological divergence* — competing realities rather than competing interpretations.

### The Tiered Internet Risk

A likely technical adaptation to these pressures is stratification: verified, authenticated, curated networks
alongside open, high-noise, low-verification networks. This is a reasonable engineering and market response.

The deeper concern is what stratification implies socially. If access to trustworthy information carries a cost,
and if that divide aligns with economic and political divides, the result is *epistemic class inequality*.
Populations in manipulation-prone information environments become more susceptible to radicalization,
misinformation, and political exploitation. That is a civilizational concern, not a technical one.

### Collapse Is Gradient, Not Binary

Most systemic failures are not cliffs. Trust erodes unevenly across domains. Institutions degrade at different
rates. Local trust can persist even as national trust weakens. The key variable is not whether degradation occurs
but whether it outpaces adaptation.

Slow degradation allows norm formation, legal reform, technical countermeasures, and cultural adjustment. Fast
degradation produces panic, polarization, and reactionary governance. History suggests societies generally survive
technological transitions when change is paced. The printing press destabilized Europe. Radio amplified propaganda.
Television centralized narrative control. Social media fragmented it. Each shift produced instability before
equilibrium. We are still in the destabilization phase.

### The Determining Variable

Technologies do not determine outcomes. Incentives and norms do.

The printing press produced both religious wars and the Enlightenment. The difference was not the press. It was
institutions and norms. The question for AI is not whether the capability exists. It is whether governance,
virtue, and institutional integrity keep pace with the rate of exploitation.

I do not trust most national governments to manage this transition well without significant upheaval first — not
out of disloyalty, but out of structural realism. Democratic governments are reactive by design. They lag
innovation, frame any intervention as partisan, and operate on short political timelines. I believe in the rule
of law. I do not believe in the rule of parties, personalities, or pandering personas. Authoritarian governments
may move faster, but at the cost of liberty. Neither is a reassuring model.

What I do trust is civil society — and specifically, faith communities — within the scope of their influence.
Not to regulate the internet, which is beyond their reach, but to operate at the *character layer*. They can
teach discernment. They can reinforce norms of truth-telling. They can build resilient communities that are harder
to manipulate. They can model the long-term, service-oriented accountability that institutions require to remain
trustworthy.

Families, churches, local communities, professional guilds, and academic institutions are historically the
mediating structures between state and individual. When civil society is strong, polarization is dampened.
When it weakens, tribalism accelerates.

The stabilizing force is unlikely to be a government regulation. It will be whether enough people, shaped by
enough communities of virtue, choose stewardship over extraction.

## Implications

- **Epistemically**: the burden of trust is shifting from "I saw it" to "can this be authenticated?" —
  cryptographic provenance and verified identity systems will become critical infrastructure
- **Economically**: scarcity of trust makes trust-bearing institutions more valuable; signal becomes premium
  when noise dominates
- **Socially**: epistemic inequality, if it aligns with economic strata, amplifies existing political instability
  and makes populations more susceptible to manipulation
- **Personally**: the character layer matters more, not less, as the information environment degrades;
  discernment is a virtue that requires deliberate cultivation

## Open Questions

- Will authentication infrastructure (cryptographic signing, media provenance standards) be adopted at a scale
  and speed that matters before trust erosion becomes entrenched?
- Does a tiered internet become a durable stratification, or a transitional phase before broader verification
  becomes standard?
- Can civil society remain coherent and influential enough to act as a stabilizing force, or is it itself
  being fragmented by the same dynamics it is meant to counter?
- Is the rate of institutional reform sufficient to outpace the rate of epistemic decay?
- What does it look like for an individual to live well and think clearly in a high-noise, low-trust
  information environment?
